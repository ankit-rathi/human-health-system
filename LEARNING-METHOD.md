# Learning Method and Content Production

## 1. Teaching philosophy

Teach for understanding, not information volume.

A learner should be able to explain an idea in their own words, connect it to another concept, apply it to a simple example and recognise the limits of what can be concluded.

Use plain language first. Introduce technical terminology when it adds precision, and explain it immediately.

The method is **visual-first, not visual-only**. A strong explanation should work as a picture, a short piece of writing, a spoken lesson and a practical example.

## 2. The capsule: smallest reusable learning unit

A capsule is the smallest canonical unit of learning. It can become part of a book, course, article, visual explainer or video.

Recommended structure:

1. **Capsule ID and title** — stable identity and a clear name.
2. **Fundamental question** — the question the capsule resolves.
3. **One-sentence insight** — memorable, intuitive summary.
4. **Visual mental model** — a diagram, sketch, flow or comparison.
5. **Three short explanatory paragraphs** — the accessible core explanation.
6. **What happens biologically?** — mechanism and necessary terminology.
7. **Everyday example** — a recognisable situation.
8. **Connection to the wider system** — links to other processes or foundations.
9. **Practical relevance** — what the learner can reasonably understand or consider.
10. **Common misconception** — what is often misunderstood.
11. **Evidence and limitations** — what supports the explanation, for whom, and what remains uncertain.
12. **Check your understanding** — questions that test comprehension.
13. **Dependencies and next question** — where the capsule sits in the learning journey.
14. **Sources, author and review date** — traceability and maintenance.

The three-paragraph explanation is the entry point, not a restriction on supporting detail.

## 3. Capsule quality: explain, connect, qualify

Each capsule should do three jobs:

- **Explain:** make the central concept understandable.
- **Connect:** show how it relates to the body, another process or a lifestyle influence.
- **Qualify:** show what the evidence does and does not justify.

For example, a capsule on insulin should not jump directly from a simplified mechanism to a universal dietary rule. It should explain insulin's normal roles, how regulation works, where the mechanism fits into whole-body metabolism, what different measurements can indicate, and where clinical interpretation is required.

## 4. Visual-first, not visual-only

Use a visual when it makes a relationship easier to understand:

- Process diagrams for sequences and feedback loops.
- System maps for interactions.
- Comparisons for similarities and differences.
- Timelines for change over time.
- Scales or ranges for variation and uncertainty.
- Simple metaphors when they clarify without distorting the biology.

Avoid decorative visuals, false precision and diagrams that imply a single cause where several factors interact.

Each concept should also have a concise written explanation, a spoken explanation or video where useful, an everyday example and a question that checks understanding.

## 5. One source capsule, multiple formats

Create one reliable, researched source capsule and adapt it into:

- Visual sketch-note.
- Short video or reel.
- Social post.
- Detailed article.
- Longer video lesson.
- Book or course section.
- Workshop exercise or discussion prompt.

Do not research the same topic independently for every platform. Maintain one canonical source and link derivative content back to it. Derivative formats may simplify the explanation, but must not change its meaning or remove important safety context.

## 6. Research workflow

1. Define the question precisely.
2. Identify the claim type: definition, mechanism, association, intervention, recommendation, safety, diagnosis or lived experience.
3. Identify the relevant population and context.
4. Search current guidelines, systematic reviews and primary research as appropriate.
5. Examine study design, outcomes, limitations, conflicts of interest and date.
6. Compare sources; do not rely on one paper, one expert or a social-media summary.
7. Separate what is observed, what is inferred and what is recommended.
8. Draft in plain language without overstating certainty.
9. Seek qualified review for clinical, safety-sensitive or potentially misleading claims.
10. Publish traceable sources and a review date.
11. Revisit content when important evidence or guidance changes.

## 7. Teaching quality checks

Before publishing, ask:

- Can a non-specialist explain the main idea after reading or watching?
- Does the visual accurately represent the concept?
- Have I explained normal function before dysfunction?
- Have I explained the mechanism without overstating it?
- Have I separated association, causation and intervention evidence?
- Have I explained who the evidence applies to?
- Have I avoided implying that one marker or pathway explains everything?
- Have I explained important uncertainty and limitations?
- Could a reader mistake this for diagnosis or treatment instruction?
- Does the capsule connect to the wider learning journey?
- Are the sources credible, traceable and current enough for the claim?

## 8. Assessment: test understanding, not recall

Useful question types:

- Explain the idea in your own words.
- Draw or describe the process.
- Identify how two systems interact.
- Apply the concept to a hypothetical everyday example.
- Explain what cannot be concluded from a measurement.
- Compare a popular claim with the available evidence.
- Identify what additional information would be needed.
- Recognise when a question should be referred to a qualified professional.

## 9. Editorial voice

- Clear, curious, candid and respectful.
- First-principles, but never needlessly academic.
- Visual and intuitive, without sacrificing accuracy.
- Practical, without promising outcomes.
- Comfortable saying "we do not know" or "the evidence is mixed."
- No guru language, fear-based hooks, miracle claims or anti-medicine rhetoric.
- No false dichotomy between lifestyle and appropriate medical care.

## 10. Content status

Use simple labels in working notes:

- `idea` — possible topic, not researched.
- `researching` — sources being reviewed.
- `draft` — explanation in progress.
- `review` — awaiting evidence or professional review.
- `published` — released to learners.
- `revisit` — evidence or guidance may have changed.

## 11. Capsule completion test

A capsule is ready when a learner can answer:

1. What is the concept?
2. How does it work?
3. Why does it matter?
4. What does it connect to?
5. What does the evidence support?
6. What should I not conclude from it?
7. What is the next useful question?
