# Learning Method and Content Production

## 1. Teaching philosophy

Teach for understanding, not information volume.

The learner should be able to explain an idea in their own words,
connect it to another concept and recognise when it applies. Avoid
polished-sounding complexity when a simple explanation is possible.

Use plain language first. Introduce technical terminology when it adds
precision, and explain it immediately.

## 2. The capsule

A capsule is the smallest reusable unit of learning. It can become part
of a book, course, article, visual explainer or video.

Recommended capsule structure:

1.  **Capsule ID and title**
2.  **Fundamental question**
3.  **One-sentence insight** --- an intuitive and memorable summary
4.  **Visual mental model** --- a sketch, diagram or analogy
5.  **Three short explanatory paragraphs**
6.  **What happens biologically?** --- mechanism and necessary
    terminology
7.  **Everyday example** --- a recognisable situation
8.  **Practical relevance** --- what the learner can reasonably take
    away
9.  **Common misconception** --- what is often misunderstood
10. **Evidence and limitations** --- strength, uncertainty and
    applicability
11. **Check your understanding** --- one or more questions
12. **Dependencies and next question** --- where this capsule fits in
    the curriculum
13. **Sources and review date**

The three-paragraph explanation is the entry point, not a restriction on
the depth of the supporting material.

## 3. Visual-first, not visual-only

Visuals are a core teaching method, but learners differ. Each concept
should be understandable through:

-   A visual explanation.
-   A concise written explanation.
-   A spoken explanation or video where useful.
-   A practical example.
-   A question that tests comprehension.

A visual should explain a relationship, process, comparison or
structure. Avoid decorative diagrams that add no understanding.

## 4. Content multiplication

Create one reliable source capsule, then adapt it into multiple formats.

**One researched concept →** - Visual sketch-note - Short video / reel -
LinkedIn or social post - Detailed article - Longer video lesson - Book
or course section

Do not research the same topic independently for every platform.
Maintain one canonical version and link derivative content back to it.

## 5. Research workflow

1.  Define the question precisely.
2.  Identify what kind of claim it is: biological mechanism,
    association, intervention, recommendation, safety or lived
    experience.
3.  Search for relevant guidelines, systematic reviews and primary
    research.
4.  Check population, context, study design, limitations and date.
5.  Compare sources; do not rely on one paper or a social-media summary.
6.  Draft the explanation in plain language.
7.  Separate established findings from uncertainty and interpretation.
8.  Seek qualified review for clinical, safety-sensitive or potentially
    misleading claims.
9.  Publish sources and review date.
10. Revisit material when important evidence or guidance changes.

## 6. Teaching quality checks

Before publishing, ask:

-   Can a non-specialist explain the main idea after reading this?
-   Is the central visual accurate and easy to interpret?
-   Have I explained the mechanism without overstating it?
-   Have I separated association from causation?
-   Have I avoided universal claims where individual variation matters?
-   Have I explained important limitations?
-   Could a reader mistake this for a diagnosis or treatment
    instruction?
-   Are the sources credible and traceable?
-   Does the capsule connect to the larger learning journey?

## 7. Assessment

Assess understanding, not just recall.

Useful question types: - Explain the idea in your own words. - Identify
the relationship between two systems. - Interpret a simple hypothetical
example. - Identify what cannot be concluded from a measurement. -
Compare a popular claim with the available evidence. - Decide when a
question should be referred to a qualified professional.

## 8. Editorial voice

-   Clear, curious, candid and respectful.
-   First-principles, but never needlessly academic.
-   Visual and intuitive, without sacrificing accuracy.
-   Practical, without promising outcomes.
-   Comfortable saying "we do not know" or "the evidence is mixed."
-   No guru language, fear-based hooks or miracle claims.

## 9. Content status

Use simple labels in working notes:

-   `idea` --- possible topic, not researched
-   `researching` --- sources being reviewed
-   `draft` --- explanation in progress
-   `review` --- awaiting evidence or professional review
-   `published` --- released to learners
-   `revisit` --- evidence or guidance may have changed
