# Curriculum: The Human Health System

## Learning objective

Move learners from fragmented health information to a connected, practical understanding of human health.

The curriculum progresses from the meaning of health, to the body's organisation and regulation, to the influences that shape health, and finally to informed action and lifelong learning.

The six parts are a learning sequence. They are not six independent compartments: concepts introduced early should reappear in later parts and be connected across the curriculum.

## The learning journey at a glance

1. **Understanding Health** — What are we trying to understand and improve?
2. **Understanding the Human Body** — What is the body, and how is it organised?
3. **Understanding How the Body Works** — Which processes keep the body functioning and adapting?
4. **Understanding What Influences Health** — How do everyday behaviours and environments affect the body?
5. **Connecting the System and Making Decisions** — How do influences, measurements and outcomes fit together?
6. **Managing Health Across Life** — How do we build habits, use healthcare and keep learning?

## Part I — Understanding Health

**Central question:** What do we mean by health, and how can we understand it?

Topics:

- Health, well-being, disease, risk, function and resilience.
- Health as a changing state rather than a perfect or fixed condition.
- Prevention, screening, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation: different purposes.
- Individual health versus population-level evidence.
- Determinants of health: biology, behaviour, relationships, environment and access to care.
- Health literacy and evaluating information.
- Risk, uncertainty, correlation, causation and absolute versus relative change.
- What a health measurement represents—and what it cannot establish by itself.

**Learner outcome:** Explain health as multidimensional and dynamic, and distinguish a measurement, a risk estimate, an interpretation and a clinical diagnosis.

## Part II — Understanding the Human Body

**Central question:** What is the body made of, and how are its parts organised?

Topics:

- Cells, tissues, organs and organ systems.
- The body's major systems and their roles.
- Blood, circulation and transport.
- Digestion, absorption and elimination.
- Brain, nerves, hormones and communication.
- Muscles, bones, joints and movement.
- Immune defence and tissue repair.
- Genes, development, ageing and individual variation.
- How structure and function are related.

**Learner outcome:** Describe the major body systems and explain why no organ or system operates in isolation.

## Part III — Understanding How the Body Works

**Central question:** Which biological processes keep the body stable, responsive and adaptable?

This part builds the bridge between anatomy and lifestyle. It explains normal function before introducing dysfunction. The mechanisms below are important teaching lenses, not a claim that they are the only processes that matter or that one process is the root cause of all disease.

### 1. Regulation and homeostasis

- Internal balance and the limits of the idea of a fixed "set point".
- Feedback loops: sensors, signals, responses and adaptation.
- Nervous and hormonal regulation.
- Circadian rhythms and time-dependent biology.
- Stress responses and recovery.

### 2. Energy and metabolism

- Energy intake, expenditure, storage and use.
- Glucose regulation and the roles of insulin and other hormones.
- Fat metabolism and energy storage.
- Liver, muscle, adipose tissue and metabolic communication.
- Metabolic flexibility and adaptation.
- Why metabolism cannot be reduced to one hormone or one blood test.

### 3. Cellular maintenance and adaptation

- Mitochondria and cellular energy production.
- Protein synthesis, repair and turnover.
- Autophagy as one of several cellular maintenance processes.
- Oxidative stress and antioxidant defence.
- Inflammation as a normal defence and repair process, and the distinction between acute and chronic inflammation.
- Glycation and advanced glycation end-products.
- Cell membranes, receptors and signalling.
- Epigenetics: how gene activity can respond to biological and environmental signals.

### 4. Integrated physiology

- How the cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, digestive, endocrine, nervous and immune systems interact.
- Fluid, electrolyte and temperature regulation.
- Muscle and bone adaptation.
- Sleep, appetite, energy and hormonal signalling.
- Ageing, repair capacity and individual differences.

**Learner outcome:** Explain selected biological processes in plain language, understand how they interact, and recognise the difference between a plausible mechanism and a proven clinical outcome.

## Part IV — Understanding What Influences Health

**Central question:** How do everyday inputs, behaviours and environments influence the body's functioning?

### 1. Nutrition

- Energy needs and dietary patterns.
- Carbohydrates, proteins and fats: roles, types and context.
- Fibre, micronutrients and food quality.
- Food processing, dietary variety and meal composition.
- Digestion, gut function and hydration.
- Meal timing and eating patterns: what is known and what remains context-dependent.
- Supplements: evidence, indications, interactions and safety.
- Dietary preferences, culture, affordability and access.

### 2. Movement and physical capacity

- Physical activity, exercise and sedentary behaviour.
- Cardiovascular fitness and endurance.
- Strength, muscle mass and function.
- Mobility, balance and coordination.
- Bone health and mechanical loading.
- Exercise adaptation, recovery and progressive overload.
- Safe participation, accessibility and professional support.

### 3. Sleep and recovery

- Sleep stages and architecture.
- Circadian rhythm and light exposure.
- Sleep quantity, quality and regularity.
- Recovery, fatigue and adaptation.
- Common sleep disruptors and when assessment may be needed.

### 4. Mind, stress and mental well-being

- Stress physiology and individual responses.
- Emotional regulation and coping.
- Breathing, relaxation and mindfulness: potential uses and limitations.
- Mental well-being, psychological health and social context.
- When qualified mental-health support is appropriate.

### 5. Relationships and social context

- Social connection and health.
- Family, work, community and caregiving.
- Culture, routines, support and accountability.
- Socioeconomic circumstances and access to healthy choices.

### 6. Environment

- Air quality, heat, noise and the built environment.
- Food environments and availability.
- Light, outdoor activity and nature.
- Work patterns, technology and digital environments.
- Safety, transport and access to healthcare.

**Learner outcome:** Explain how multiple influences shape health and why recommendations need to account for context, feasibility, risks and individual variation.

## Part V — Connecting the System and Making Decisions

**Central question:** How do body processes, lifestyle influences, measurements and outcomes fit together?

Topics:

- Energy balance, appetite, body composition and adaptation.
- Glucose regulation, insulin action and metabolic health.
- Blood pressure, vascular function and cardiovascular risk.
- Lipids and the difference between biomarkers and clinical outcomes.
- Muscle, bone, mobility and healthy ageing.
- Gut function, nutrition and the wider body.
- Sleep, stress, appetite, activity and recovery as interacting influences.
- Inflammation: biological role, measurement and limitations.
- Multi-factor risk: why a single cause or marker rarely tells the whole story.
- Understanding common measurements: blood pressure, glucose, HbA1c, lipids, waist, body composition and fitness measures.
- Baselines, trends, variability, measurement error and reference ranges.
- Evaluating a health claim, intervention or success story.
- Weighing benefits, harms, uncertainty, cost and personal context.
- Knowing when a question requires clinical assessment.

**Learner outcome:** Build a connected explanation of a health question, interpret common information cautiously and make better-informed decisions with appropriate professional support.

## Part VI — Managing Health Across Life

**Central question:** How do we turn understanding into sustainable habits and navigate a changing health landscape?

Topics:

- Behaviour change, motivation, identity and habit formation.
- Goal-setting and realistic feedback loops.
- Designing environments and routines that make healthy choices easier.
- Planning for setbacks, travel, family and work constraints.
- Preventive care, screening and vaccination literacy.
- Preparing for healthcare appointments and shared decision-making.
- Understanding treatment plans and asking informed questions.
- Working with qualified professionals and seeking a second opinion when appropriate.
- Healthy ageing, function and independence.
- Wearables, personal health data, privacy and data quality.
- Artificial intelligence in health: opportunities, risks and limitations.
- Misinformation, marketing, conflicts of interest and changing evidence.
- Lifelong learning and periodic review of personal routines.

**Learner outcome:** Build and adapt general health habits, use healthcare more effectively and remain thoughtful as evidence and technology evolve.

## Cross-cutting mental models

These ideas should recur throughout the curriculum:

1. **The body is interconnected:** changes in one system can influence others.
2. **Regulation is dynamic:** the body adapts; it is not a machine with one fixed ideal number.
3. **Context matters:** age, health status, environment, resources and preferences affect what is appropriate.
4. **Mechanism is not outcome:** explaining how something might work does not prove that it improves health.
5. **Measurements are partial views:** every test has a purpose, limitations and uncertainty.
6. **Patterns matter:** long-term behaviours and exposures usually matter more than isolated actions.
7. **Health decisions involve trade-offs:** benefits, risks, feasibility and personal values all matter.
8. **Professional care is part of health management:** education supports care; it does not replace it.

## Curriculum design rules

- Teach prerequisites before advanced concepts.
- Explain normal function before dysfunction.
- Avoid presenting one biological pathway as the universal root cause of complex conditions.
- Connect every lifestyle foundation to relevant body processes and other foundations.
- Include everyday examples and practical context.
- Distinguish general education from individual medical advice.
- Give more depth to concepts with greater explanatory value.
- Revisit key concepts from different perspectives.
- Include evidence, uncertainty and limitations alongside practical relevance.
- Review content when important evidence or guidance changes.

## Chapter continuity contract

Every chapter should answer:

1. What fundamental question does this chapter answer?
2. What should the learner be able to explain afterwards?
3. Why does this knowledge matter in everyday life?
4. Which earlier concept does it depend on?
5. Which other body system or lifestyle factor does it connect to?
6. What question naturally leads to the next chapter?

A chapter is complete when it advances the learner's mental model—not merely when it contains a collection of facts.
