# Evidence, Credibility and Professional Scope

## 1. The trust principle

Health education can influence consequential decisions. Accuracy,
context and transparency matter more than confidence, popularity or
persuasive storytelling.

The platform's credibility must come from the quality of its
explanations, the traceability of its sources, its willingness to
acknowledge uncertainty and its respect for professional boundaries.

## 2. Evidence hierarchy is a guide, not a shortcut

Prefer, where relevant:

1.  Current evidence-based clinical or public-health guidelines from
    reputable bodies.
2.  Systematic reviews and meta-analyses with appropriate methods.
3.  Well-designed randomised trials for intervention questions.
4.  Strong observational studies for associations and long-term
    patterns.
5.  Mechanistic and laboratory research for biological plausibility.
6.  Expert consensus and established textbooks for foundational
    explanations.

No hierarchy removes the need to examine study quality, population,
outcomes, conflicts of interest, uncertainty and relevance to the
question.

A mechanistic explanation does not by itself prove that an intervention
improves real-world health outcomes.

## 3. Label the nature of claims

When useful, distinguish:

-   **Established:** supported by consistent, relevant evidence or
    authoritative guidance.
-   **Supported but contextual:** evidence exists, but the effect
    depends on population, dose, setting or other factors.
-   **Emerging:** promising but not yet sufficiently established.
-   **Uncertain or mixed:** credible evidence points in different
    directions or is insufficient.
-   **Traditional or experiential:** part of a tradition or personal
    experience; not automatically established by clinical evidence.
-   **Unsupported:** evidence does not justify the claim as presented.

These are editorial descriptions, not formal scientific grading systems.

## 4. Rules for communicating evidence

-   Distinguish correlation from causation.
-   Distinguish relative risk from absolute risk where relevant.
-   Explain the population studied and avoid assuming results apply to
    everyone.
-   Do not turn animal, cell or mechanistic findings into proven human
    benefits.
-   Do not treat testimonials as proof of efficacy.
-   Avoid claims that a food, supplement, detox, fasting protocol or
    exercise cures disease unless supported by appropriate evidence and
    within the relevant professional context.
-   Do not imply that a single biomarker tells the whole story.
-   Explain measurement variability and limitations.
-   State when evidence is evolving or uncertain.
-   Disclose sponsorships, affiliate links and conflicts of interest.

## 5. Professional identity and boundaries

The intended identity is **health educator, author and platform
founder**.

General health education may include explaining anatomy, physiology,
nutrition concepts, physical activity, sleep, stress, health literacy,
evidence and general preventive-health principles.

The platform must not present the founder or unregistered collaborators
as clinicians or regulated professionals where they are not.

Without the relevant qualifications and registration, do not: - Diagnose
a disease or interpret a person's symptoms as a clinical diagnosis. -
Prescribe or change medication. - Provide individualised treatment for a
medical condition. - Present therapeutic diets as clinical treatment. -
Provide physiotherapy, rehabilitation or injury treatment. - Claim
authority to practise a regulated profession. - Replace or discourage
appropriate medical care.

A disclaimer does not make an otherwise out-of-scope activity
permissible.

## 6. Clinical collaboration

Build relationships with appropriately qualified and registered
professionals, as relevant:

-   Medical practitioners for diagnosis, medical assessment and
    treatment.
-   Registered dietitians or other appropriately recognised nutrition
    professionals for clinical nutrition.
-   Qualified exercise professionals for general fitness programming
    within competence.
-   Physiotherapists for rehabilitation and clinical movement needs.
-   Psychologists or other qualified mental-health professionals for
    mental-health care.

Partnerships should define: - Each person's qualifications and
responsibilities. - Which content requires clinical review. - Referral
criteria and urgent escalation. - Informed consent and privacy. - How
personal information is collected, stored and shared. - How conflicts of
interest and commercial arrangements are disclosed.

Do not imply that a professional has endorsed the entire platform merely
because they reviewed one item.

## 7. Personal experience

The founder's health journey may provide motivation, context and
relatable examples.

It is not a clinical trial and does not prove that a method will work
for others.

Keep personal health records private. Obtain explicit consent before
sharing another person's health story, image, testimonial or data. Avoid
turning personal measurements into public medical advice.

## 8. Health data and AI

Future tools may support: - General health literacy. - Habit reflection
and tracking. - Educational explanations of common measurements. -
Preparation of questions for healthcare appointments. - Source discovery
and summarisation with human review.

They must not be positioned as independent diagnostic, prescribing or
treatment systems.

Protect privacy, minimise data collection, obtain meaningful consent,
explain limitations and seek appropriate legal and clinical governance
before handling sensitive health data or launching health-related
technology.

## 9. Review and accountability

Every substantive educational item should have: - Sources. - Author or
editor. - Date created and last reviewed. - A note where evidence is
uncertain or context-dependent. - Professional review where the risk or
subject matter warrants it.

Correct material transparently when it is wrong or outdated. Trust is
strengthened by responsible correction, not by pretending errors never
happen.
