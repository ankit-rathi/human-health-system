# Evidence, Credibility and Professional Scope

## 1. The trust principle

Health education can influence consequential decisions. Accuracy, context and transparency matter more than confidence, popularity or persuasive storytelling.

The platform earns credibility through clear explanations, traceable sources, honest uncertainty, responsible correction and respect for professional boundaries.

The purpose of this document is not to make the founder a clinician. It is to make the platform a responsible educator.

## 2. Evaluate the claim before evaluating the source

First identify what kind of statement is being made:

- **Foundational fact:** for example, the role of an organ or nutrient.
- **Mechanistic claim:** how a biological process may work.
- **Association:** two variables are observed to vary together.
- **Causal claim:** changing one factor produces a change in another.
- **Intervention claim:** a particular action improves an outcome.
- **Clinical recommendation:** what should be done for a person or population.
- **Safety claim:** whether an action may cause harm or interact with treatment.
- **Experience or testimonial:** what happened to an individual or group.

Different claims require different evidence. A laboratory mechanism may support plausibility, but it does not establish that a diet, supplement or protocol prevents or reverses a disease.

## 3. Use an evidence hierarchy thoughtfully

Depending on the question, prioritise:

1. Current evidence-based clinical or public-health guidelines from reputable bodies.
2. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses with appropriate methods.
3. Well-designed randomised trials for intervention questions.
4. Strong observational studies for associations and long-term patterns.
5. Mechanistic and laboratory research for biological plausibility.
6. Expert consensus and established textbooks for foundational explanations.

This is a guide, not an automatic ranking. Study quality, population, outcomes, bias, conflicts of interest, uncertainty and relevance all matter. A systematic review of weak studies does not become strong evidence merely by being a review.

## 4. Distinguish evidence from interpretation

Use these editorial labels where helpful:

- **Established:** supported by consistent, relevant evidence or authoritative guidance.
- **Supported but contextual:** evidence exists, but effect depends on population, dose, setting or other factors.
- **Emerging:** promising but not sufficiently established.
- **Uncertain or mixed:** credible evidence is conflicting or insufficient.
- **Traditional or experiential:** part of a tradition or personal experience; not automatically established by clinical evidence.
- **Unsupported:** evidence does not justify the claim as presented.

These are editorial descriptions, not formal scientific grading systems.

## 5. Rules for communicating health claims

- Distinguish correlation from causation.
- Distinguish relative risk from absolute risk where relevant.
- Explain the population studied and avoid assuming findings apply to everyone.
- Do not turn animal, cell or mechanistic findings into proven human benefits.
- Do not treat testimonials or before-and-after stories as proof of efficacy.
- Do not imply that a biomarker is a diagnosis or a complete measure of health.
- Avoid universal "root cause" explanations for complex conditions.
- Avoid claiming that a food, supplement, detox, fasting pattern or exercise cures disease unless the claim is supported by appropriate evidence and communicated within the relevant professional context.
- Do not imply that a change in a surrogate marker necessarily means fewer symptoms, complications or clinical events.
- Explain measurement variability, reference ranges and limitations.
- State when evidence is evolving, mixed or absent.
- Disclose sponsorships, affiliate links, financial interests and relevant conflicts.

## 6. How to handle frameworks from other organisations

External programmes can be useful sources of questions, teaching ideas and service-design patterns. They are not automatically validated scientific models.

When reviewing a framework such as a multi-pillar lifestyle programme:

1. Separate its educational structure from its biological claims.
2. Extract useful questions and concepts without adopting its conclusions.
3. Trace each important claim to the underlying research or guideline.
4. Check whether cited studies actually support the stated conclusion.
5. Distinguish programme-reported outcomes from independently established effectiveness.
6. Treat case studies as illustrative, not proof of generalisable results.
7. Do not reproduce medical protocols, medication-tapering rules or target ranges as general advice.
8. Record what is supported, uncertain, disputed or outside the platform's scope.

A programme's use of medical terminology, publication counts or conference presentations does not by itself validate every claim it makes.

## 7. Professional identity and boundaries

The intended identity is **health educator, author and platform founder**.

General education may explain anatomy, physiology, nutrition concepts, physical activity, sleep, stress, health literacy, research and general preventive-health principles.

Without relevant qualifications and registration, the founder and platform must not:

- Diagnose disease or interpret an individual's symptoms as a clinical diagnosis.
- Prescribe, stop or change medication or recommend medication tapering.
- Provide individualised treatment for a medical condition.
- Present therapeutic diets as clinical treatment.
- Provide physiotherapy, rehabilitation or injury treatment.
- Claim authority to practise a regulated profession.
- Tell people to discontinue or avoid appropriate medical care.
- Use disclaimers to disguise otherwise out-of-scope activity.

A disclaimer does not make an otherwise out-of-scope activity permissible.

## 8. Clinical collaboration

Build relationships with appropriately qualified and registered professionals, as relevant:

- Medical practitioners for assessment, diagnosis and treatment.
- Registered dietitians or other appropriately recognised nutrition professionals for clinical nutrition.
- Qualified exercise professionals for general fitness programming within competence.
- Physiotherapists for rehabilitation and clinical movement needs.
- Psychologists or other qualified mental-health professionals for mental-health care.

Define responsibilities, review requirements, referral criteria, urgent escalation, informed consent, privacy, data handling, commercial arrangements and conflicts of interest.

Do not imply that a professional endorses the entire platform because they reviewed one item. Name the scope and date of each review.

## 9. Personal experience and programme outcomes

The founder's health journey may provide motivation and relatable examples. It is not a clinical trial and does not prove that a method works for others.

For external programme outcomes, ask:

- Who collected the data?
- How were participants selected?
- How many enrolled, completed and were lost to follow-up?
- Were outcomes independently verified?
- Were medication changes clinician-supervised?
- Were adverse events reported?
- Was there a comparison group?
- How long were outcomes maintained?

Do not turn selected success stories or provider-reported totals into promises or estimates of what a new participant can expect.

Obtain explicit consent before sharing another person's health story, image, testimonial or data. Keep the founder's personal health records private unless there is a clear, deliberate reason to share them.

## 10. Health data and AI

Future tools may support general health literacy, habit reflection, educational explanations of common measurements, appointment preparation and source discovery with human review.

They must not be positioned as independent diagnostic, prescribing or treatment systems.

Minimise data collection, protect privacy, obtain meaningful consent, explain limitations and seek appropriate legal and clinical governance before handling sensitive health data or launching health-related technology.

## 11. Review and accountability

Every substantive educational item should record:

- Sources and relevant publication/guideline dates.
- Author or editor.
- Date created and last reviewed.
- Claim type and evidence status where useful.
- Important uncertainty, context and limitations.
- Professional reviewer and review scope where applicable.

Correct material transparently when it is wrong or outdated. Trust is strengthened by responsible correction, not by pretending errors never happen.
